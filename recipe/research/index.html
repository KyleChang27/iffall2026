Recipe Research

Fig Leaf
Ice Cream

A custard-style ice cream infused with toasted fig leaves, which give the cream a delicate fig and coconut-like aroma. Served with fresh figs and pistachios, it feels botanical, restrained, and distinctly late-summer-to-fall.

Source: Beyond Sweet & Savory 8 servings 15 min prep + 20 min cook, plus steeping/chilling/freezing

01

Ingredients

Ice cream base

  • 2 oz fresh fig leaves, washed and dried
  • 2½ cups heavy cream
  • 1 cup full-fat milk
  • ½ cup granulated sugar, divided
  • 4 large egg yolks

For serving

  • 1 pint fresh figs, quartered
  • ¼ cup chopped pistachios

02

Instructions

  1. Heat the oven to 325°F. Toast the fig leaves directly on the center rack for about 8 minutes, until dry and fragrant but not burned.
  2. Warm the milk, cream, and ¼ cup sugar over low heat. Crumble in the toasted leaves and keep warm for 5 minutes without letting the mixture boil.
  3. Remove from heat, cover, and steep for at least 2 hours. For a stronger infusion, chill and steep overnight, up to 8 hours.
  4. Strain the infused dairy into a clean saucepan and gently bring it back to a simmer.
  5. Whisk the egg yolks with the remaining sugar until pale. Slowly whisk in small amounts of hot cream to temper the yolks, then return everything to the saucepan.
  6. Cook over medium-low heat, stirring constantly, until the custard thickens enough to coat a spatula, about 15 minutes. Strain again.
  7. Cool the base in an ice bath, then refrigerate for at least 4 hours or overnight.
  8. Churn in an ice cream machine until soft-serve texture, about 25–30 minutes. Transfer to a container and freeze until firm, at least 4 hours.
  9. Serve with quartered fresh figs and chopped pistachios.

Recipe Website Research

References for hierarchy, usability, and how food photography can coexist with dense recipe information.

01

NYT Cooking

NYT Cooking has a strong editorial hierarchy: large food photography draws attention first, while titles, metadata, ingredients, and instructions remain easy to scan. I would like to borrow that balance of visual restraint and practical clarity rather than making the recipe feel like a long blog post.

02

Food52

Food52 feels warmer and more lifestyle-oriented, with photography carrying much of the personality of the page. I like how the site can feel editorial and domestic at the same time; that tone could work well for a recipe centered on an unusual seasonal ingredient like fig leaves.

03

Serious Eats

Serious Eats is useful as a functional reference because the information is organized around actually cooking the recipe. Its clear sectioning and emphasis on technique suggest a way to make the long steeping, custard, chilling, and churning process easier to follow without oversimplifying it.

05

Non-Recipe Website Research

Visual and communication references for a botanical, quiet, editorial direction.

01

Aesop

Aesop uses botanical subject matter without relying on obvious decorative “nature” graphics. The restrained typography, muted palette, and generous spacing make ingredient-focused content feel considered; I could use a similar approach to keep fig leaves central without making the page overly rustic.

02

FRAMA

FRAMA’s site combines neutral space, material photography, and a rigid editorial grid. I like the way quiet colors allow texture to become the main visual element, which could translate well to close-up photographs of fig leaves, cream, ceramic, and pistachios.

03

Kinfolk

Kinfolk treats images and typography like a magazine rather than a standard utility website. Its pacing—large image moments followed by compact text—could help the recipe feel more atmospheric while still giving the ingredients and steps enough space to remain readable.
